How is travel time calculated?

We divide the average distance between the Sun and the planet by the speed of light in a vacuum (299,792.458 km/s). The result is the one-way travel time in seconds. The distance changes along each orbit, so the result is an estimate.

Origins

How did the solar system form?

The solar system consists of the Sun and everything bound to it by gravity. About 4.6 billion years ago, the material that would become Earth, the other planets and the Sun was scattered through a cloud of gas and dust.

01 · The cloud

Gravity drew matter together

One region of the cloud began to contract under its own gravity. As it shrank, it spun faster. Much of the material fell toward the center, while the rest settled into a surrounding disk.

02 · The star

The Sun began to shine

Gas at the center was compressed and heated. Once the core reached the necessary temperature and pressure, nuclear fusion began. Hydrogen nuclei combined to form helium, releasing energy. The same process powers the Sun today.

03 · The planets

Dust grains grew

Grains collided in the disk, and some stuck together. Over time they formed bodies several kilometers across, called planetesimals. The largest attracted more material through gravity. This gradual growth, called accretion, produced planetary embryos.

04 · Two regions

Temperature shaped the planets

Near the Sun, it was too hot for ice to survive. Rock and metal supplied the building material for the four inner planets. Farther out, solid ice was available too, giving growing planets more material to collect. Jupiter and Saturn also captured large amounts of hydrogen and helium.

How does this relate to the Big Bang?

The Big Bang describes the expansion of the universe from a hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago. By the time the solar system began to form, the universe was already about 9.2 billion years old.

How do we know when it happened?

Some meteorites preserve minerals that solidified in the original disk. Certain atoms in these minerals decay at known rates. By measuring how much of the original material remains and how much has changed, scientists can date those early solids.

Skywatching

A month on the Moon

The Sun lights roughly half the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of its sunlit half. The cycle from one new moon to the next takes about 29.5 days.

Space exploration

What the probes saw

1989 · Neptune

Voyager 2

After visiting Jupiter, Saturn and Uranus, Voyager 2 reached Neptune. Its images revealed storms in the atmosphere and dark plumes rising above the icy surface of Triton.

2005 · Titan

Cassini and Huygens

Cassini carried Huygens to Saturn. As Huygens descended through Titan’s thick atmosphere, it photographed channels below. After landing, it revealed ground strewn with rounded pieces of ice.

2015 · Pluto

New Horizons

The first flyby of Pluto revealed mountains of water ice beside a plain of nitrogen ice. The scarcity of craters in parts of the plain suggests that its surface has been renewed in the recent geological past.

Atlas guide

Beyond the planets

What keeps planets in orbit?

The Sun’s gravity pulls on the planets as they travel through space, bending their paths into ellipses. One complete orbit defines a year on each planet. Rotation is a separate motion: a planet spinning on its own axis.

What reaches us from the Sun?

Light from the Sun’s visible surface takes about eight minutes to reach Earth. The Sun also releases charged particles in a stream called the solar wind. When these particles interact with Earth’s magnetic field and atmosphere, auroras can appear near the poles.

How do asteroids and comets differ?

Asteroids consist mostly of rock or metal. Many orbit between Mars and Jupiter. Comets also contain ice. As a comet approaches the Sun, some ice turns directly into gas, releasing dust. A cloud forms around the nucleus, and tails can extend away from the Sun.

Where are Ceres and Pluto?

Both are dwarf planets. They orbit the Sun and their gravity has pulled them into nearly round shapes, but they share their orbital neighborhoods with other substantial bodies. Ceres lies in the asteroid belt. Pluto orbits in the Kuiper Belt, a region rich in icy bodies beyond Neptune.

What might lie beyond the Kuiper Belt?

The orbits of some long-period comets point to a much more distant origin. Astronomers propose that a huge shell of icy objects surrounds the solar system: the Oort Cloud. Its existence is inferred from these orbits and has yet to be observed directly.
